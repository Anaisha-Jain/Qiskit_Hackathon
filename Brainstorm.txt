Problem: Protons can tunnel between DNA base pairs and briefly form rare tautomers, a proposed source of spontaneous point mutations.
 This is quantum biology with cancer implications.
Bridge: Input a base pair (A-T or G-C) and get a double-well potential, discretized onto qubits.
 Simulate proton transfer dynamics and tunneling probability under different conditions (temperature, nearby drug molecules).
Quantum part: Hamiltonian simulation with Trotterization, and optionally open-system dynamics with a Lindblad or ancilla-based model.

Resources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39069038/


Image: the image shows a simulated proton trying to move from its normal position to the “wrong” position in a DNA base pair. That wrong position is the tautomer.
    Right panel (the important one)
    The proton starts in its normal spot, the left well.
    The y-axis is the probability of finding it in the wrong spot, the right well.
    The x-axis is time.
    Blue line (symmetric): the two spots have equal energy, so the proton swings back and forth between them and reaches about 99%. This is the idealized case.
    Orange line (asymmetric): the wrong spot has higher energy, like a real base pair where the normal form is much more stable. The proton only reaches about 17%. This is closer to real DNA, where the rare form is just that, rare.

    So the plot reads as: “the more the wrong position is disfavored, the less likely the proton is to show up there.” In your finished tool, A-T and G-C would each get their own curve, and you’d compare how likely each is to enter the tautomer form.
    Left panel (the setup behind it)
    It’s the energy landscape the proton lives in: two dips (normal and wrong position) separated by a hill (the barrier). The colored lines are the proton’s lowest-energy quantum states. They sit below the top of the hill, which is the point: the proton shouldn’t get over, but quantum mechanics lets it tunnel through.
    Conclusion: bigger energy difference between the wells means a lower chance of the proton reaching the wrong spot.

Real physics instead of placeholders. Pull barrier heights and well asymmetry for A-T and G-C from the proton-transfer literature, convert to real units (energy, distance, proton mass), and cite your sources. A tool that outputs tunneling timescales for actual base pairs is much more convincing than dimensionless toy numbers. Most of the effort is reading papers and doing the unit conversion carefully.
Temperature and decoherence (open quantum system). The plain model oscillates forever, which real biology doesn’t. Add a noise or dissipation channel (density-matrix simulation, then a Qiskit version with ancilla qubits or Aer noise) and output a tunneling rate versus temperature. This is the physics the real research on this topic cares about, and it addresses the limitation I flagged in the pitch. It’s the hardest upgrade and the most impressive.
A second quantum algorithm. Use VQE plus an excited-state method (SSVQE or qEOM) to compute the tunneling splitting directly, then compare it with Trotter dynamics and exact diagonalization. Two algorithms cross-checked against each other looks like real methodology.
Two protons at once. A-T has two hydrogen bonds and G-C has three. Model coupled proton transfer in 2D (for example 2 × 4 = 8 qubits). The Hamiltonian grows, the circuits get deeper, and a scaling study of qubits versus gate count versus classical cost makes a better “why quantum” slide.
A real hardware run. Run a small instance on an IBM QPU with error mitigation and compare it against the simulator. This is low-effort for a high wow factor, as long as you keep the circuit shallow.
